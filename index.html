Care guide

General information, not a diagnosis.

Hygiene during your period
  • Change a pad every 4 to 6 hours, even if it is not full. A tampon should stay in 8 hours at most, and a menstrual cup up to 12 hours.
  • Wash your hands before and after changing.
  • Wash the outside of the genital area with plain water or a mild unscented soap once or twice a day. Do not wash or douche inside the vagina.
  • After using the toilet, wipe from front to back.
  • Wear clean, breathable cotton underwear and change it daily, or sooner if it is damp or stained.
  • Wrap used pads in paper and put them in a bin. Do not flush them.
  • Wash reusable cloth pads in hot water and dry them fully in the sun. Boil a menstrual cup for 5 to 10 minutes between periods.
  • Skip scented pads, sprays and powders. They can irritate the skin.
  • It is fine to bathe, wash your hair and exercise during your period.
  • Drink water, eat regular meals with iron-rich foods, and rest when you can.
Urine and discharge

Urine

  • Pale yellow to light amber is normal. Darker yellow or orange usually means you need more water.
  • Bright yellow is often from B vitamins. Beetroot and some medicines can turn urine pink or reddish for a day or two.
  • Period blood does not change the colour of urine. You may see blood in the toilet that comes from the vagina. Pink or red urine when you are not on your period needs a doctor.
  • See a doctor for cloudy urine, a strong smell, burning or pain when peeing, needing to pee very often, or blood in the urine. These can be signs of a urine infection, which is common and easy to treat.

Discharge through the cycle

  • After your period: little, white or cloudy, a bit sticky.
  • Fertile window: clear, slippery and stretchy, like raw egg white.
  • Before your period: thicker, creamy white or cloudy.
  • Brown or dark discharge at the start or end of a period is usually old blood.
  • Green, grey or yellow discharge, a fishy or foul smell, itching or burning needs a check.
When to see a doctor
  • Soaking a pad every hour for several hours, or passing clots bigger than a coin.
  • A period that lasts more than 7 days.
  • Cycles that are regularly shorter than 21 days or longer than 35 days, or no period for 3 months when you are not pregnant.
  • Pain that stops you from school, work or daily life, or pain that keeps getting worse.
  • Bleeding between periods or after sex.
  • Fever, dizziness, vomiting or a rash while using a tampon or cup. Take it out and get urgent help.
  • A late period when you could be pregnant. Take a pregnancy test from the first day of the missed period, and see a doctor if it is positive or you are unsure.
